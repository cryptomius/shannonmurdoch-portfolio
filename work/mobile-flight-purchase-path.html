Case study 02 · Webjet (ASX:WEB)

Six rounds of testing to de-risk a mobile purchase path

Webjet’s domestic flights mobile website, rebuilt from agency wireframes the flight API couldn’t serve. In a purchase path, confusion converts directly into lost revenue — so nothing reached build on my judgement alone. Including the fix I took to test twice, and customers rejected twice.

Roles I covered
UX researcher, interaction designer, UI designer, prototyper, design-system implementer
Team
Me as UX lead, with an external agency (initial wireframes), the UI dev team, and Product & Marketing stakeholders
Duration
Six consecutive rounds of remote usability studies, all of them before build
Tools
SketchInVision UserTesting.comUI Pattern Library
Outcome
Two confusion points that had survived three rounds eliminated; a fully specified, evidence-backed design that went to build

01

What I inherited: two products that had drifted apart

Webjet reached smartphone customers through a mobile web site and an iOS app that had been built separately. Search for a flight and what you got depended entirely on where you happened to land.

Mobile web presented results as a dense sortable table — depart time, stops, price — then sent you off to a separate screen to choose a fare type.

The iOS app used a different visual language entirely, and kept fare selection inside the flight detail screen.

How I framed my job on this

Flight results is the screen where a customer decides whether to buy. Every moment of hesitation in it — not knowing which leg you’re choosing, not trusting the order of the list, not knowing how to back out — is a chance to abandon. So I treated the project as de-risking a revenue-generating purchase path rather than redesigning a page. That framing decides what you do with disagreement: my opinion is a hypothesis, and the only thing that settles it is watching customers use the interface.

02

The agency wireframes, and the constraint they missed

An external agency had produced wireframes before I was involved. Much of the interface they proposed could not be accomplished within the constraints of single API call content retrieval — the data simply wasn’t there to draw those screens.

The fare comparison also leaned on iconography to express the differences between fares. That was ambiguous to read, and it carried more development work than the MVP allowed.

Two ideas in there were worth keeping. Cabin classes surfaced per flight rather than buried, and — more importantly — the card expanding in place, so choosing a fare happened on the results page. Our existing mobile site handed customers off to a separate screen to pick a fare; keeping them in the list was obviously better. Where exactly those two ideas should live took me two more passes to settle.

Decision: adapt rather than reject

Binning the agency work outright would have been the easy call, and it would have thrown away the one structural idea worth keeping. Instead I separated the concepts from the execution: keep what the data contract supports, rebuild the rest against what the API actually returns. Finding this out at wireframe stage costs an afternoon. Finding it out mid-sprint costs a sprint.

03

Two wireframe passes before anyone saw it

Pass one reconciled the agency structure with reality: depart and return tabs, a filter and sort strip, one top-level card per cabin class, and card expansion to reveal fare types and flight details.

Then I critiqued my own pass. Expansion to see fares was good practice and stayed. But giving each cabin class its own top-level card was neither necessary nor clear — it meant one flight appearing repeatedly down the list, which is exactly the kind of thing that makes a customer doubt they’re reading the list correctly.

Pass two simplified the top-level cards and moved every cabin class into the expanded card. It also called out non-standard airports by airport code, and introduced a new approach: grouping paired fares into their own cards at the top level.

General flow approved. Now it was worth building a prototype.

04

From wireframe to clickable prototype

High-fidelity mocks next, then clickable prototypes in InVision for remote usability testing. Two things went in at this stage that hadn’t been in the wireframes:

  • Non-primary airports highlighted orange. Avalon is not Tullamarine, and a customer who discovers that at the airport is a customer you have lost.
  • Marketing and Product requirements — a featured airline slot and fare rules. The out-of-scope ‘compare fares’ button came out for MVP.

Then a round of Webjet-side changes before testing:

  • Filter and Sort combined into one control. Customers used the two words interchangeably, so I stopped making them choose between them.
  • A change-of-mind pathway surfaced in the UI — ‘Edit Search’ given a permanent home rather than being a back-button exercise.
  • ‘Selected Orange’ from the pattern library for the expanded card state, along with buttons and notifications.
  • Visual hierarchy tidied so the card’s text elements sat in the correct order of importance, and ‘via’ city names plus stop times added.
  • Seven card types identified and designed: featured, sale, non-stop, with stops, non-standard airport, next-day arrival, and operated by another carrier.
  • A larger call to action.

Design system, doing its job

Buttons, notifications and the selected-card colour all came from the UI Pattern Library I’d founded the year before. That is six rounds of studies in which nobody had to argue about a button, and six rounds in which the front-end team could read the prototype as components they already had. A design system pays for itself in the projects it makes boring.

05

The paired flight flow, borrowed rather than reinvented

Some fares only allow a return on a specific set of flights with the same airline. On desktop this had been a purchase-path dead-end until we fixed it, and the studies that fixed it had already established what customers could understand.

So I designed the mobile paired flight flow on those learnings rather than starting fresh, and adopted the ‘paired flight’ nomenclature from the earlier studies for consistency. Every key variant flow was explicitly designed rather than left for build to interpret: results collapsed and expanded, and the returning-leg results collapsed and expanded.

Decision: test the case where the customer is disappointed

Round one ran three scenarios: a one-way booking, a paired flight the customer wanted, and a paired flight the customer did not want. Rounds two to six ran the last two. Designing the happy path is the easy half; the revenue leaks out of the moment a customer is told they can’t have what they picked. If the recovery path isn’t tested, it isn’t designed.

06

What earns its pixels

Travel products are information-dense and a phone gives you one column, so the last pass before testing was about what each element was actually worth.

  • Repetition reduced. Departure and arrival airports hidden unless the airport was non-primary — the customer told us the route, so restating it on every card is noise. The exception is exactly the case where it matters.
  • Next-day arrivals called out explicitly, in orange. Arriving on a different date than you assumed is a refund request waiting to happen.
  • Card height reduced, so more product sits above the fold.

I also showed the near-final card to the UI dev team and took their feedback: a full-height price box, duration and stops onto a single line, airport codes dropped to save space, and more padding top and bottom. Every one of those made the card easier to scan as well as easier to build — which is what you would hope for from feedback given by the people who have to build it.

07

Six rounds, decided by customers

Each round: remote usability studies through UserTesting.com, observations written up, changes made, retest. Two problems refused to die for three straight rounds — and the fix I chose for a third problem failed twice.

1

The observations, and the assumption I logged

What happened

  • The header wasn’t sticky — the filter button scrolled out of sight exactly when people wanted it
  • Most participants wanted to filter and sort, but not all of them clicked the blue ‘Filter & Sort’ button. People clicked Edit Search first
  • Wayfinding needed work: the departing-to-returning transition was abrupt, the next step was unclear initially, and it wasn’t clear which direction of flight you were selecting
  • ‘Select ›’ didn’t read as the path forward to see paired returning flights
  • Paired flights should be visible as soon as the first paired card is expanded — or instructions added to say how to reach them
  • FEATURED was throwing people off as to what the sort order was
  • A couple of participants avoided even expanding paired flight options, reading them as less choice
  • One participant suggested reversing the header on the returning leg to ‘Auckland to Melbourne’

What I changed

  • Made the header, including the filter button, sticky
  • Pulled the returning flight details out of the next screen and into the same card, so people didn’t have to leave the results — a call we made as a team, mirroring the desktop matrix
  • Began experimenting on FEATURED, again as a team: a dividing line, then a red background tint, then — eventually — a rename

The assumption I logged

  • The transition between departing and returning was a quick fade, and the tabs alone weren’t telling people what to do next. I assumed making the header sticky would go some way to resolving this. Writing that down is what made round two informative rather than merely disappointing.
2

The assumption fails, and so does my fix

What I changed

  • Sticky header
  • Background tint on the featured card, with the sort-order line moved below it to divide it from the list
  • Single-card paired flight — departing and returning legs combined into one expanded card
  • ‘Departing Flight’ / ‘Returning Flight’ headings on expanded cards
  • Removed the count of paired returning flights, to see whether hiding a small number encouraged engagement

What happened

  • FEATURED and sort-order confusion: unchanged
  • Which-leg-am-I-on confusion: unchanged, despite the sticky header. My assumption from round one was wrong
  • The single-card paired flight flow was very poor. Confusion all around, information overload, no clarity on what to do if the return flight wasn’t wanted — participants were trying to un-tick items to escape
  • Hiding the count changed the rate of engagement not at all, so the number of return options went back in
3

Explaining harder didn’t work either

What I changed

  • Removed the depart/return tabs entirely
  • Added an imperative at the top of the results telling people what to do
  • Added explanation and instructions to the expanded paired flight card

What happened

  • FEATURED and sort order: still confusing
  • Which leg am I on: still confusing, imperative or not
  • Paired flight flow improved…
  • …but still very poorly understood. Not an acceptable solution

The call

  • Revisit the original paired flight flow rather than keep patching the combined card, and address the rest with header changes, screen transitions, and clarifying the next step on the paired flight card
4

Reverting, renaming, and both problems gone

What I changed

  • Rebuilt the header: sortable column titles, the imperative, and the date
  • FEATURED renamed PROMOTION
  • Paired flight reverted to the original flow; ‘Select’ relabelled ‘Continue’
  • Switched the positions of Filter and Edit Search
  • Loading screens added between the departing and returning steps — even though the content was already loaded, because participants expected a transition

What happened

  • FEATURED and sort-order confusion: eliminated
  • Which-leg confusion: eliminated, by keeping a single imperative visible at all times in the header — focusing people on the task in front of them instead of asking them to hold a two-step model
  • Paired flight experience greatly improved
5

Carrying the decision forward

What I changed

  • Edit Search and Filters moved to a floating footer that shows and hides itself automatically as cards expand
  • The selected departing flight now displayed on the returning results screen

What happened

  • Confusion about which flight had been selected in the previous step: eliminated
  • Paired flight clarity improved
6

Last-mile language

What I changed

  • Paired flight icon added to the selected flight strip
  • ‘Don’t like your choices?’ became ‘Want more flight options?’, with a clear back arrow and call to action
  • ‘Paired flight:’ prefix added to the explanation text

What happened

  • Paired flight clarity improved again

The observation I deliberately did not act on

In round one, a couple of participants avoided expanding paired flight options because they read them as offering less choice. That is an interesting signal and it would have been easy to redesign around it. But it was a couple of people. Not enough participants to warrant a change, so I logged it for future testing and left it alone. Being led by evidence has to include not over-reading it — otherwise every round spends its budget chasing the last participant who said something memorable.

08

The design that went to build

Sortable column headers with a single imperative and the date pinned above them. PROMOTION instead of FEATURED. Paired flights on the original two-step flow with clearer labelling and a positively framed way out. Filters and Edit Search in a floating footer that gets out of the way. The selected departing flight carried forward so nobody has to hold it in their head.

Seven card types and every key paired-flight variant flow specified, with pattern-library components already in place for the front-end team. It went to build and shipped.

Outcome

Both confusion points that had survived three rounds of iteration were eliminated, and the paired flight experience went from confusion all around — participants trying to un-tick items — to clarity improving in each of the last three rounds. The design reached the development team already carrying its evidence, which is the point. Every one of the six rounds cost a fraction of what it costs to discover the same problem in production, on the screen where customers are deciding whether to buy.

09

What I learned

Five things I took from this

  • Write your assumptions down before you test them. I recorded that I expected a sticky header to go some way to fixing the wayfinding problem. It didn’t, and because the expectation was on paper, round two produced a finding instead of a shrug. An untested assumption is a risk you are carrying without pricing it.
  • Be willing to undo your own work. The single-card paired flight was the fix I took to test, and customers rejected it twice. Reverting in round four is what made rounds four, five and six productive. Having a standing research budget is what made reverting survivable rather than embarrassing.
  • One honest word did more than two rounds of visual treatment. On FEATURED we tried a dividing line, then a red background tint. Round four renamed it PROMOTION and put sortable column titles in the header, and the confusion was gone. My read is that the label had been making a claim about the ordering of the list that it couldn’t support, so no amount of contrast was ever going to fix it.
  • People expect a transition even when there is nothing to wait for. The content was already loaded, and the quick fade between departing and returning wasn’t telling participants what had just happened or what to do next — they expected a transition. Adding a loading screen with nothing to load is a counterintuitive fix I’ve reused since.
  • Framing changes the relationship. “Don’t like your choices?” became “Want more flight options?” and paired flight clarity improved again. My read is that the negative version quietly implied the customer had made a mistake, where the positive version reads as an offer — same link, same destination, different tone of voice.

The through-line, and the reason I work this way: on a purchase path, my taste doesn’t get the deciding vote. Some of the changes I was most confident about moved nothing, the two problems I believed were solved in round two weren’t solved until round four, and the paired flight approach I advocated had to be taken back out. Every one of those was cheap to find in a prototype. Finding the same things later, in a conversion chart, means the money has already left.
