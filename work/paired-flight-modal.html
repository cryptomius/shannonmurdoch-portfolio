Case study 01 · Webjet (ASX:WEB)

Unblocking a purchase-path dead-end worth half a million a month

Customers who selected a paired international fare landed on a screen where every returning flight appeared unavailable. The business had circled this for two years with three unbuilt solutions on the table.

01

A dead end that looked like a bug

When someone searched for a return flight, Webjet displayed the results in one of three different ways depending on the route and what the airlines made available:

  • Domestic Matrix — one-way fares out, one-way fares back. Mix and match freely.
  • International Matrix & Hybrid Ribbon — paired fares only. You must return with the same carrier, on the flights the airline has fixed to that outbound.
  • Hybrid Matrix — both kinds in the same grid, which is where trans-Tasman routes landed.

Paired fares were marked with a small ‘R’ icon. Select one, and most prices in the returning matrix greyed out — because only a handful of returning flights were valid with that fare. Nothing explained this. Customers had to hunt the grid for the one matching price.

The real problem

This wasn’t a layout issue. Customers concluded the site was broken or sold out, and left. A purchase path that silently punishes people for choosing the cheapest fare is a revenue problem wearing a usability costume.

02

What I inherited: three solutions, none built

Normally I’d open a project like this with a discovery phase. Here I didn’t need to — and saying so mattered, because the team’s patience for another round of research on a known problem was thin. The product team had already discussed it at length. Sketched proposals were sitting on the server from June 2014, eight months before I joined.

Solution C was the direction the product team chose, and they mocked it up in January 2015. Then it stalled — because it was a significant change to a revenue-critical flow, and nobody could say with confidence that customers would understand it.

Decision: skip discovery, spend the time on validation

The problem was well understood and a solution direction already had internal agreement. Re-running discovery would have burnt credibility for no new information. What was missing was evidence that people could actually use it — so I moved the entire research budget for this project downstream into prototype testing.

03

How I approached it

I picked the project up in March 2016, while working through a broader set of usability issues in the matrix results interface. First move was a mockup built to UI Pattern Library standards — buttons, notifications and type all conformant, so that the thing we tested was the thing engineering could actually build without a spec argument.

The team ran on the Double Diamond, which I’d embedded as our working methodology. The part that earns its keep is not the shape — it’s the explicit separation of divergent from convergent thinking. Without it, fledgling ideas get killed with “we can’t do that” before anyone has established whether we can.

04

Prototype and task design

Two scenarios, run back to back. We hadn’t tested the Matrix much, so scenario one was a plain cheapest-flight task that would give us general feedback on the whole interface. Scenario two was the one that mattered.

Decision: test the worst case, not the happy path

Scenario two deliberately forced people into the modal and guaranteed it would not contain a flight matching their brief. Anyone can design a modal that works when the answer is inside it. The whole failure mode we were fixing was what happens when it isn’t — so that’s what I put in front of people.

Then the tasks. Writing these is the part people underestimate: you cannot hand participants knowledge an average customer wouldn’t have, or you test your own explanation rather than the interface. I also used the opening task to capture their mental model — asking what factors would drive their choice before they saw any UI. Where the interface and the mental model diverge is where the usability problems live.

The final task revealed what we’d been testing and invited them to say what they thought of it. That last prompt has produced some of the most useful comments I’ve ever collected.

05

Four rounds, and the round that broke the deadlock

Each round: unmoderated remote studies through UserTesting.com, observations written up, changes made, retest. Two of these rounds told me my fix hadn’t worked.

1

The pattern-library mockup

What I tested

  • Modal on fare selection, ‘R’ icon retained, icon description and instructions present

What happened

  • People were confused about why they were seeing the modal, and what to do if they didn’t like the options — the same failure as the flow we were replacing
  • “Was the total $254, or 2 × $254?”
  • The ‘R’ icon meant nothing. Nobody read the description or the instructions

Read

  • Steve Krug’s line from Don’t Make Me Think — “people don’t read, they scan” — was doing a lot of work in that debrief
2

Big explainer strip

What I changed

  • Added a prominent strip across the top explaining the ‘R’ and what to do if the options didn’t suit
  • Prefixed the second price with ‘+’

What happened

  • The ‘+$254’ killed the total-versus-extra confusion outright
  • “Return fare” didn’t resonate — to customers every flight they were booking was a return fare. We needed a different word
  • Prominent or not, people still didn’t read it. Too wordy
3

More direction (the wrong lever)

What I changed

  • Added more direction to the explanation and italicised the next steps

What happened

  • Still unread. Still confused about why the modal appeared
  • Too much to absorb at a glance — I had responded to “they don’t understand” by writing more words, which is exactly the wrong instinct
4

New word, split message

What I changed

  • Renamed the concept: ‘R’ for Return Flight became ‘P’ for Paired Flight
  • Split one block of copy into two separate parts — what this is, and what to do if you don’t want it

What happened

  • “Paired flight” landed. It was a new term, so people had no competing prior meaning to fight
  • Separating explanation from next-step meant each was short enough to be scanned rather than skipped

The insight that unlocked it

Three rounds of failure were all the same failure: I was trying to explain an unfamiliar concept using a familiar phrase. “Return fare” already meant something to customers, so my explanation was competing with their existing definition and losing. Inventing “paired flight” gave the concept a label with no prior meaning attached — and suddenly the explanation had somewhere to land. The nomenclature outlived the project and became the standard term across Webjet’s flight products.

06

The design that shipped

Round four validated the concept. The remaining work was craft: making vertical space earn its keep, and handling the messy real-world cases the mockups had quietly ignored.

  • Higher-contrast next-step instruction — the one line that had to survive scanning
  • Compressed vertically so more flight options sat above the fold
  • Details pop-over on hover, keeping full flight detail available without occupying permanent space
  • Peeking for three or more options — show half a row plus the scrollbar so people know to keep going
  • Accommodated multiple flight numbers, carriers and aircraft types per pairing, which the earlier mockups had all assumed away
07

What it was worth

Event tracking went in with the interface, so we could read the conversion shift by matrix type rather than as one blended number. The split matters, because it tells you the change did what we said it would.

Outcome

≈ +$500,000 AUD in total transaction value every month, from resolving a single usability dead-end. The uplift was twice as large where the modal was unavoidable, which is exactly what you’d predict if the modal was the thing that changed — the pattern of the result is as reassuring as the size of it.

The research also travelled. The paired-flight insights and terminology fed straight into the mobile flights redesign that ran alongside it, which saved that project several rounds of relearning the same lesson on a smaller screen.

08

What I’d have done next

Shipping is not finishing. Three enhancements were specified and queued behind other work:

  • Make opening and closing the modal instant, with no page scroll. A platform limitation at the time, and the single biggest remaining friction.
  • Turn dismissed paired fares purple — the web-standard visited-link colour — so people don’t reopen options they’ve already rejected. Cheap, and it removes real cognitive load.
  • Combine matrix columns for same-flight paired fares. Two fares departing at the same time on the same carrier don’t need two columns; merging them reduces the horizontal sprawl that makes the matrix hard to scan in the first place.
09

What I learned

Four things I took from this

  • When people don’t understand, adding words makes it worse. I ran that experiment twice before I accepted it. Rounds two and three were both “explain harder”, and both failed. The fix was fewer words and a better one.
  • Borrowed vocabulary competes with the meaning people already have. A new concept sometimes needs a new name. “Paired flight” worked precisely because nobody arrived with a definition of it.
  • Test the failure case. The scenario where the answer isn’t in the modal was where all the value was. A happy-path test would have passed in round one and shipped the same dead end with nicer typography.
  • Instrument before you launch, and segment the result. A blended conversion number would have been arguable. Two segments moving in proportion to how often people saw the change is a much harder result to talk your way out of — in either direction.
